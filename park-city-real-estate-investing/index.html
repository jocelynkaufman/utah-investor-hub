The Park City Market

Real Estate Investing in Park City

Park City is Utah’s resort market — an appreciation-first town where property values ride on two ski resorts, Sundance each January, and a well-heeled second-home buyer base. Investors come here for luxury short-term rentals, long-term appreciation, and sometimes a 1031-exchange parking spot. It is not where first-time Utah investors should start.

Nightly-rental zoning is tight, HOA rules bite harder than most newcomers expect, and entry prices are the highest in the state. The investors who do well here go in knowing that Park City is not a cash-flow market the way Salt Lake or Ogden can be. It’s a long-horizon appreciation and lifestyle asset, and the strategy has to match.

The Character of the Market

What Makes Park City Unique for Investors

Park City lives and dies by tourism. The Park City Mountain and Deer Valley resorts anchor winter demand, the Sundance Film Festival spikes January traffic, and summer events plus trail access keep the shoulder seasons busier than most mountain towns. That gives nightly rental properties a long usable season — but only if your specific address is zoned to allow nightly rentals in the first place.

Nightly-rental licensing in and around Park City is strictly regulated and tends to tighten rather than loosen. Different HOAs permit different things. Some condo developments were purpose-built for nightly rentals and operate as de facto hotels. Others allow 30-day minimums only. Others prohibit short-term rentals entirely. For an investor, the first job in Park City is not finding a deal — it’s confirming that the deal you’re looking at can legally be run as the business you’re planning to run.

The second feature is the buyer profile. Most Park City real estate is bought by people who intend to use it themselves and rent it when they’re not. That drives prices well above what rental income alone would support. If you’re modeling Park City purely on cash flow against a mortgage, the numbers almost never work. Where the math does work is on long-horizon appreciation, tax-advantaged depreciation from a short-term rental treated as an active business, and the optionality of a property you might one day want to live in.

Best Strategies for Park City

Four strategies that actually fit a resort market — chosen for where the Park City tax, licensing, and appreciation profile pulls in the same direction.

The Classic Park City Play

Short-Term Rentals in Park City

If your target property sits inside a nightly-rental-approved zone or HOA, a Park City STR can run as a legitimate hospitality business — ski-season peaks, summer trails, Sundance spikes. Expect operational intensity (cleanings, pricing, guest comms) and expect to verify licensing before you commit.

Pull Deductions Forward

Cost Segregation on a Park City STR

High-basis properties plus short-term rental tax treatment is where cost segregation pays its biggest dividends. A professionally run cost-seg study on a Park City STR can pull a large chunk of depreciation forward into year one — genuinely meaningful cash savings for high-income owners.

The Non-Nightly Alternative

Long-Term Rentals in Park City

For properties that don’t qualify for nightly rentals, long-term leases to resort staff, remote workers, and seasonal professionals are the fallback. Rents are strong, tenant quality is high, but you’re unlikely to beat STR gross on any zoned-STR property.

Luxury Renovation

Fix & Flip in Park City

Park City flips look nothing like a Wasatch Front cosmetic redo. Luxury finish standards, long permitting timelines, and tighter buyer pools all apply. Experienced flippers with real capital can still find value-add plays in older Old Town cabins and dated condos on the ski-in/ski-out side.

What to Buy Here

Rental Property Types That Work in Park City

Nightly-rental-zoned condo near a resort base

The highest and best STR play. Condos purpose-built for short-term rental with HOAs that permit (and in some cases manage) nightly guests are the easiest path into Park City hospitality cash flow.

Old Town cabin with STR history

Historic Old Town homes with existing nightly-rental licensing carry a scarcity premium. Guests want character; the town wants the preservation. Both work in the investor’s favor if operational standards are met.

Townhome in a mixed-use development

Newer townhome communities often draw clearer HOA lines between nightly-permitted and long-term-only buildings. Lower per-unit maintenance, more predictable rules.

Single-family home in a long-term-only zone

For investors who want the appreciation story without the STR headache, a long-term SFH rental in the Snyderville Basin or near the Jordanelle can anchor a high-net-worth tenant and let appreciation compound quietly.

Resort-managed property as a hands-off asset

Some Park City developments are run as rental pools by the resort itself. The gross yield is usually lower than running your own STR, but the operational burden is close to zero — a reasonable fit for an investor who lives out of state.
