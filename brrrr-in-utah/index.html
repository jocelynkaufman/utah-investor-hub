BRRRR in Utah: Does the Strategy Still Work With Today’s Prices and Rates?

BRRRR Financing

Every few months someone asks me some version of the same question: "Does BRRRR still work in Utah, or did that window close in 2021?" It's a fair question. The strategy got popular when you could buy a tired Ogden bungalow for a song, fix it up, and refinance at a rate that started with a 3. Neither of those things is true anymore.

The honest answer is that BRRRR still works in Utah, but not the way the podcasts described it. The "pull all your money back out and do it again in six months" version is rare right now. A slower, more patient version still works on the right property in the right part of the Wasatch Front. This guide walks through the actual refinance math at today's rates, where the numbers still have a chance, and how to tell when a deal should just be a buy-and-hold instead.

A Quick Refresher: What BRRRR Actually Requires

BRRRR stands for Buy, Rehab, Rent, Refinance, Repeat. You buy a property below its potential value, renovate it, place a tenant, then refinance based on the new appraised value (the "after repair value," or ARV) to pull out some or all of the cash you put in. That recovered cash funds the next deal.

For the cycle to work, three things have to line up:

  • A wide spread between purchase price and ARV. The forced appreciation from your rehab is what creates the equity you refinance against.
  • A refinance that pencils. Lenders cap how much of the new value you can borrow, and the rate on that new loan determines whether the property cash flows afterward.
  • Rents that cover the new, larger loan. If you pull out all your cash but the property loses money every month, you haven't built a business. You've built a liability.

If you're newer to the strategy, our overview of BRRRR investing in Utah covers the basics in more depth. Here, we'll focus on what's changed.

What's Different About BRRRR in Utah Right Now

Rates moved the goalposts

The biggest change is the refinance rate. Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey had the average 30-year fixed rate a little above 7% in mid-November 2026, and that's the average for owner-occupied loans. Investment property loans typically price higher than that average, and cash-out refinances on rentals often carry an additional pricing adjustment. Get a real quote from a lender before you underwrite a deal. Don't plug the headline rate into your spreadsheet.

Higher rates hit BRRRR twice. They raise your monthly payment after the refinance, and they shrink the pool of buyers and lenders competing for distressed properties, which can help on the purchase side. That second effect is real, but it rarely makes up for the first.

Prices didn't fall the way some expected

Utah home values have mostly held up even as rates climbed. That means the deep discounts BRRRR depends on are harder to find. They still exist, usually in properties with real problems: dated mechanicals, foundation questions, estate sales, or homes that have sat on the market because they don't show well. You have to be willing to take on those problems, and you have to vet your rehab team carefully. Our guide on how to vet contractors for rental property renovations is worth reading before you make an offer on anything that needs significant work.

Refinance rules haven't loosened

Conventional cash-out refinances on investment properties are capped well below 100% of value. Fannie Mae's limits for a one-unit investment property are generally 75% of the appraised value, and lower for two-to-four-unit buildings. Many lenders also require you to have owned the property for a set period before they'll do a cash-out refinance based on the new value, though some offer exceptions if you bought with cash. Seasoning rules vary by lender and loan program, so ask about them before you buy, not after the rehab is done. If your DTI won’t support a conventional refinance, DSCR loans let Utah investors qualify on the rental income itself, which is often how BRRRR deals actually close in this rate environment.

A Realistic Utah BRRRR Example at Today's Rates

Let's walk through a hypothetical deal. These numbers are illustrative only. They aren't a quote, an appraisal, or a forecast. Your purchase price, rehab costs, rent, and loan terms will be different, and you should run your own numbers with real comps and a real lender quote.

Say you find a dated three-bedroom bungalow in an older Ogden neighborhood:

  • Purchase price: $290,000
  • Rehab (kitchen, bath, flooring, mechanicals): $55,000
  • Closing and holding costs: $15,000
  • Total cash into the deal: $360,000
  • After repair value (ARV): $430,000
  • Market rent after rehab: $2,300 per month

Scenario 1: The "classic" BRRRR refinance

You refinance at 75% of the $430,000 ARV, which gives you a $322,500 loan. That leaves about $37,500 of your own money in the deal. That's a strong recovery of capital.

Now the cash flow. At an assumed 7.75% investment-property rate on a 30-year loan, principal and interest come to roughly $2,310 per month. Add an estimated $300 a month for property taxes and insurance and you're at about $2,610. That's before vacancy, repairs, capital expenses, or property management. Against $2,300 in rent, this property loses money every month.

That's the trap. On paper the BRRRR "worked" because you got most of your cash back. In practice, you'd be subsidizing the property out of pocket, and one bad month (a vacancy, a water heater, a slow-paying tenant) makes it worse.

Scenario 2: The "slow BRRRR"

Same property, but this time you only refinance to 60% of ARV, or $258,000. At the same assumed rate, principal and interest drop to roughly $1,848. With $300 for taxes and insurance, you're at about $2,148, which leaves a modest margin against $2,300 in rent before reserves.

The tradeoff: you leave about $102,000 in the deal instead of $37,500. You won't be repeating the cycle next quarter. But you own a renovated property with real equity, it covers its own costs, and you have the option to refinance again later if rates come down or the property appreciates.

The Slow BRRRR: How It Works in Practice

The "slow BRRRR" is less a different strategy than a different timeline. Instead of refinancing as soon as the rehab is done to pull out the maximum, you:

  1. Buy with cash, private money, or a short-term loan, the same as a classic BRRRR.
  2. Rehab and rent the property.
  3. Refinance only to the level the rent supports, which usually means a lower loan amount and more of your own money staying in the deal.
  4. Let time do some of the work. Pay down principal, raise rents at renewal where the market supports it, and watch for a lower-rate environment.
  5. Refinance again or tap equity later to fund the next acquisition.

Some investors do a version of this with a home equity line or a portfolio lender instead of a conventional cash-out. Others pair one slow BRRRR with a house hack so they're building equity on two fronts. The common thread: you're optimizing for a property that pays for itself, not for how fast you can recycle your capital.

Where BRRRR Still Has a Chance in Utah

BRRRR depends on older housing stock, a real gap between distressed and renovated values, and rents that hold up after the rehab. On the Wasatch Front, that tends to point north.

Ogden and Weber County

Ogden has some of the oldest housing stock on the Front: railroad-era bungalows, craftsman homes, and small multi-family buildings that were built for railroad-era workers a century ago. That older stock is why the gap between "needs everything" and "fully renovated" can still be wide enough to work with. Weber State University and the broader Ogden job base support rental demand. Redfin's Ogden housing market data is a good place to check current sale prices and days on market before you start making offers. For neighborhood-level detail, see our Ogden real estate investing guide and the broader Weber County guide.

Cache Valley

Logan and the surrounding Cache County towns have a mix of older homes near the core and steady rental demand tied to Utah State University. The market is smaller, so deals come up less often and comps can be thin. Pay close attention to how an appraiser will value a renovated home when there are only a few recent sales nearby, since a weak appraisal is the fastest way to break a BRRRR.

Pockets of Salt Lake and Davis Counties

Older neighborhoods in parts of Salt Lake County and Davis County can still produce BRRRR candidates, but entry prices are higher, which makes the rent-to-loan math harder. These areas often work better as a slow BRRRR than a classic one. Our Davis County investing guide covers which cities have the older stock.

A note on all of this: no area "guarantees" a deal works. The numbers on each property are what matter. A great neighborhood with the wrong purchase price is still a bad BRRRR.

When to Skip the Refinance and Just Buy and Hold

Sometimes the best BRRRR decision is to stop at the third R. Consider keeping the property as a straightforward buy-and-hold rental when:

  • The full refinance would make the property cash-flow negative. If Scenario 1 above looks like your deal, don't force it.
  • The appraisal comes in low. If the ARV you planned on doesn't hold up, refinancing to 75% of a lower number may not return enough capital to justify the new loan costs.
  • Your acquisition financing is already reasonable. If you bought with a conventional loan rather than hard money, you may not need to refinance at all right now.
  • You don't have a next deal lined up. Pulling cash out to sit in a savings account while paying a higher mortgage rate rarely makes sense.

Buy-and-hold isn't a consolation prize. A renovated rental with solid equity and positive cash flow is a strong position, and it keeps the refinance option open for later. If you're weighing that path, our guide to cash flow on your first Utah investment property walks through how to underwrite a long-term hold.

Your Utah BRRRR Checklist Before You Make an Offer

  1. Get a real lender quote for the refinance, including the rate, any investment-property or cash-out pricing adjustments, the maximum loan-to-value, and seasoning requirements.
  2. Pull renovated comps, not just active listings. Your ARV should be based on recent sales of similar renovated homes nearby.
  3. Get rehab bids before you go under contract, or at least during your due diligence period, and add a contingency for surprises in older homes.
  4. Underwrite both scenarios: a full refinance and a slow BRRRR. If neither one cash flows, walk away.
  5. Check rent comps for the renovated property, not the current condition.
  6. Know your exit if the refinance doesn't happen. Can you hold the property on your acquisition financing, or sell it without losing money?

The Bottom Line

BRRRR in Utah isn't dead, but the fast version is the exception right now, not the rule. The investors who are making it work are buying older homes in the northern Wasatch Front, underwriting conservatively at real investment-property rates, and accepting that they may leave more cash in each deal than they would have a few years ago.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does BRRRR still work in Utah?

BRRRR still works in Utah, but the 'pull all your money out in six months' version from the low-rate era is uncommon right now. A slower, more patient BRRRR — refinancing to the amount rents actually support, keeping more equity in the deal — still works on the right property in the right part of the Wasatch Front.

What is the BRRRR method?

BRRRR stands for Buy, Rehab, Rent, Refinance, Repeat. You buy a property below its potential value, renovate it, rent it, refinance against the new appraised value to recover cash, and use that cash to fund the next deal.

What makes a good BRRRR property in Utah?

The best Utah BRRRR candidates are older homes in strong rental markets with a wide spread between distressed price and renovated value — often found in Ogden, parts of Salt Lake County, and Cache Valley. Look for cosmetic and mechanical fixes that increase value more than they cost, not foundation-level rebuilds.

How do I refinance a Utah BRRRR property?

Most investors refinance with a conventional cash-out loan (typically capped at 75% of the appraised value for a one-unit investment) or a DSCR loan that qualifies on rental income. Seasoning rules — how long you have to own the property before refinancing against the new value — vary by lender, so confirm before you buy.
