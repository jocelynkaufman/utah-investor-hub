DSCR Loans in Utah: How to Qualify on Rental Income Instead of W-2s

Financing First-Time Investor

If you're self-employed, own a few rentals already, or write off enough on your tax return that your taxable income looks smaller than your real income, you've probably hit the same wall: a conventional lender looks at your W-2s and tax returns, runs your debt-to-income ratio, and tells you no. Or it tells you yes for now, but not for the next property.

That's where DSCR loans come in. A DSCR loan qualifies the property instead of you. If the rent covers the mortgage payment, the loan can work, even if your personal income documentation wouldn't get you through a conventional underwriter. This guide walks through how the math works with Utah rents, what lenders typically ask for, which Utah properties have a hard time qualifying, and when a DSCR loan is actually the better tool versus when you should stick with a conventional mortgage.

One note up front: I'm a REALTOR, not a lender. Terms change often and vary a lot from lender to lender, so treat the ranges here as a starting point for your conversations with loan officers, not as a quote.

What a DSCR Loan Is (and What It Isn't)

DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio. It's a measure of how well a property's rental income covers its monthly debt payment. Lenders that offer DSCR loans use that ratio as the main qualifying test, in place of the personal income verification you'd go through on a conventional loan.

A few things that set DSCR loans apart:

  • No W-2s, pay stubs, or personal tax returns in most programs. The lender still pulls your credit and verifies your assets and reserves, but it isn't calculating your personal debt-to-income ratio.
  • Investment property only. DSCR loans are business-purpose loans for rentals. You can't use one to buy a home you'll live in, which means they aren't a tool for a house-hacking purchase where you occupy one of the units.
  • They're non-QM loans. DSCR loans sit outside the Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines, so each lender sets its own rules. That flexibility is the whole point, but it also means two lenders can give you very different answers on the same property.
  • You can often close in an LLC. Many DSCR lenders will lend to an LLC, which conventional loans generally won't. Talk to your attorney and CPA about whether holding property in an entity makes sense for you.

The tradeoff for that flexibility is cost. DSCR loans typically carry higher rates than a conventional investment-property loan for a borrower with strong documented income, and many come with prepayment penalties. More on both below.

How DSCR Math Works With Utah Rents

The basic formula is simple:

DSCR = monthly rent ÷ monthly PITIA

PITIA is principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues (HOA). If a property rents for $2,500 a month and the full PITIA payment is $2,500, the DSCR is 1.0. The rent exactly covers the debt. If the rent is $2,750 against the same payment, the DSCR is 1.1.

Notice what's not in that formula: vacancy, repairs, capital expenses, utilities you pay, or property management. A DSCR of 1.0 means the property breaks even on the loan payment, not that it breaks even as a business. Lenders use the ratio to decide whether to lend. You still need your own full underwriting to decide whether to buy. Our guide to evaluating cash flow on a Utah investment property covers the expenses the DSCR formula leaves out.

Where the "rent" number comes from

For a purchase, the lender usually relies on the appraiser's market rent estimate. On a single-family home, that's typically a rent schedule completed alongside the appraisal. On a two-to-four-unit property, the appraiser estimates rent for each unit. If the property already has a tenant, some lenders will use the lease amount, the appraiser's market rent, or the lower of the two. Ask each lender which number it uses, because that one detail can move your ratio.

That's why it pays to check rent comps yourself before you write an offer. Zillow Research's rental data is a useful starting point for rent trends by metro and ZIP code, but nothing beats looking at what similar properties nearby have actually leased for recently.

A hypothetical Wasatch Front example

These numbers are illustrative only. They aren't a quote, an appraisal, or a forecast. Your price, rent, rate, taxes, and insurance will be different.

Say you're looking at a three-bedroom single-family rental in Salt Lake County:

  • Purchase price: $450,000
  • Appraiser's market rent: $2,500 per month
  • Assumed DSCR rate: 7.5% on a 30-year loan
  • Estimated property taxes and insurance: $325 per month
  • HOA: none

With 25% down: the loan is $337,500. Principal and interest come to roughly $2,360 a month, so PITIA is about $2,685. Divide $2,500 by $2,685 and the DSCR is about 0.93. Below 1.0.

With 30% down: the loan drops to $315,000. Principal and interest fall to roughly $2,203, PITIA to about $2,528, and the DSCR rises to about 0.99. Right at breakeven.

That's a realistic picture of a lot of Wasatch Front single-family homes right now. Prices are high relative to rents, so many properties land near or below a 1.0 ratio unless you put more money down. Some lenders will still lend below 1.0, but usually with a larger down payment, more reserves, or a higher rate. Others won't go below 1.0 at all.

What moves the ratio

  • A larger down payment shrinks the loan and the payment.
  • A lower rate, whether from better credit, a rate buydown, or shopping lenders, has a direct effect.
  • Higher rent relative to price. Small multi-family properties and homes with a legal accessory unit often produce more rent per dollar of purchase price than a single-family home.
  • Lower HOA dues and insurance. HOA dues count against you in the PITIA. A condo with high dues can fail a DSCR test that a similarly priced townhome with low dues would pass.

Typical DSCR Loan Requirements

Every lender is different, but most DSCR programs land in a similar range. Based on published lender guidelines, here's what you'll commonly see:

  • Minimum DSCR: Many lenders look for 1.0 to 1.25 for their best pricing. Some will go below 1.0 with compensating factors, and some have no stated minimum but price the loan accordingly.
  • Credit score: Minimums often start in the 620 to 680 range, and you'll usually need a higher score (often around 700 or more) for the better rate tiers and lower down payment options.
  • Down payment: Commonly 20% to 25%, with some lenders allowing less for borrowers with strong credit and more required for lower ratios, lower credit, or certain property types.
  • Reserves: Lenders typically want several months of PITIA in liquid reserves, and the requirement can grow with the number of properties you own.
  • Minimum loan amount: Many DSCR programs won't lend below a certain amount, which can rule out lower-priced properties.

Prepayment penalties

This is the term that surprises people most. Many DSCR loans include a prepayment penalty for the first few years, often on a declining schedule. If you plan to sell or refinance soon (for example, as part of a BRRRR strategy), make sure you understand the penalty before you sign. Some lenders let you choose a shorter penalty period or none at all in exchange for a higher rate.

Why Some Utah Properties Struggle to Qualify

DSCR lenders aren't just underwriting the rent. They're underwriting the property as collateral, and a few kinds of Utah properties run into trouble more often.

Rural properties and thin comps

Homes in smaller towns, on larger acreage, or in areas with few recent sales can be hard to appraise and harder to rent-comp. Some DSCR lenders exclude rural properties or properties above a certain acreage outright. Others accept them but at a lower loan-to-value. If you're looking outside the core Wasatch Front, ask about property eligibility before you spend money on an inspection.

Seasonal and short-term rental markets

A ski-season rental in Park City, a vacation property near Bear Lake, or a cabin in the mountains may earn most of its income in a few months of the year. Standard DSCR programs are built around long-term rent, and the appraiser's long-term market rent for a resort property is often far below what it earns as a short-term rental. Some lenders offer DSCR programs that use short-term rental income, based on the property's booking history or third-party revenue projections, but terms are usually stricter. Local short-term rental rules matter too, and they vary a lot by city and HOA. Our Park City investing guide covers some of what makes that market different.

Condos and HOA-heavy properties

Condos can be tricky with any loan type, and DSCR is no exception. High HOA dues pull the ratio down, and some lenders have their own rules about the condo project itself, such as how many units are rentals or whether the HOA is in litigation.

When a DSCR Loan Beats a Conventional Loan

For many first-time investors with a W-2 job and a clean financial picture, a conventional investment-property loan is the cheaper option. Our guide to financing your first investment property in Utah covers those loan types. DSCR loans start to make more sense in a few situations.

You're scaling past a handful of properties

Conventional financing gets harder as your portfolio grows. Every rental adds to your debt-to-income calculation, reserve requirements rise as you add financed properties, and Fannie Mae's multiple financed properties guidelines set stricter requirements and an overall cap on how many financed properties one borrower can have. Many investors find that somewhere past four properties, the conventional path starts to narrow. DSCR loans look at each property on its own, so your fifth or tenth rental is underwritten the same way as your first. If that's where you're headed, our guide on scaling your rental portfolio in Utah is a good companion read.

Your tax returns don't reflect your real income

Self-employed investors, business owners, and anyone with large write-offs often show less taxable income than they actually earn. That's good for your tax bill and bad for a conventional loan application. A DSCR loan sidesteps the problem by not using your returns at all.

You want to close in an LLC or move quickly

With less personal documentation to collect and verify, DSCR loans can sometimes close faster, and many lenders allow LLC borrowers. Both can help when you're competing for a deal.

When conventional still wins

  • Your income documentation is strong and your debt-to-income ratio has room.
  • It's your first or second rental and you're not near the financed-property limits.
  • You want to avoid a prepayment penalty.
  • You plan to live in the property, even part of it.

How to Shop for a DSCR Loan in Utah

  1. Get quotes from at least three lenders, including at least one mortgage broker who works with multiple DSCR investors. Pricing varies more than people expect.
  2. Ask the same questions every time: minimum DSCR, how they calculate rent, rate at your credit score and down payment, prepayment penalty options, reserve requirements, minimum loan amount, and property types they won't finance.
  3. Run the DSCR yourself before you offer. Use a conservative rent estimate and real tax and insurance figures for the specific property, not county averages.
  4. Check eligibility early if the property is rural, a condo, a short-term rental, or anything unusual.
  5. Underwrite the full deal, not just the ratio. A property that passes at 1.0 still needs to cover vacancy, repairs, and capital expenses out of something.

The Bottom Line

DSCR loans aren't a shortcut or a better version of a conventional mortgage. They're a different tool, and they cost more. But for self-employed investors and anyone scaling past a few rentals, they can open doors that conventional underwriting keeps closed. The key is going in with your own numbers: real rent comps, a real lender quote, and a clear-eyed look at whether the property works as a business, not just whether it squeaks past a ratio.

Your next step: pick one property you're watching, pull three rent comps, and calculate the DSCR at 20%, 25%, and 30% down using a quoted rate.
